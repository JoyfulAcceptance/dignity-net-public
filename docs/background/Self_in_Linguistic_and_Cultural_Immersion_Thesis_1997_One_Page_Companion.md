# One-Page Companion

## The Self in Linguistic and Cultural Immersion

**Genevieve Prentice, Marlboro College, 1997**

This thesis began as a study of second-language acquisition and became an inquiry into selfhood: what happens to a person when they enter a linguistic and cultural environment strong enough to reshape memory, expression, belonging, and identity.

The work is grounded in lived experience. After a transformative year of immersion in Denmark, where Danish became socially inhabitable and identity-expanding, I later entered Japan with far less linguistic preparation and encountered a different condition: overwhelm, blocked participation, embarrassment, and difficulty entering the language as a livable medium. The contrast between these two experiences became the center of the inquiry.

The thesis argues that language learning cannot be understood as code acquisition alone. Formal linguistic theory, especially when separated from pragmatics, context, relation, and lived use, fails to account for what matters most to the person undergoing immersion. Language is not a neutral tool. It carries selfhood, social position, memory, intimacy, and the possibility of being recognized or shut out.

The project draws on second-language-acquisition theory, pragmatics, personal interviews, autobiography, and theories of selfhood, including William James, George Herbert Mead, Robert Kegan, and David Hume. Its central movement is from language as structure toward language as lived relation. The question becomes not only "How is language acquired?" but "How does the self change when the world of meaning changes around it?"

Several themes in the thesis continue directly into my current work on human-AI interaction and governance:

- **Immersion changes the self.** A person inside a strong language environment is not merely using a system; they are being reorganized by it.
- **Theory must answer to lived experience.** Abstract accounts become inadequate when they cannot describe what happens to actual humans under real conditions.
- **Language is identity-bearing.** Voice, style, memory, and belonging are part of expression, not decorative surface.
- **Relation matters.** Meaning arises in context, use, audience, and participation, not in isolated symbols alone.
- **Coherence can be supported or destabilized.** Some environments help a person enter and become more fully themselves; others overload, fragment, or block participation.

In retrospect, the thesis is an early root of my current work. Then, the question was: what happens to the self in linguistic and cultural immersion? Now, the question has become: what happens to the human in sustained AI interaction?

The substrate changed. The question did not.
